Quodlibeta deepening · the height of self-reference

Incompleteness, measured in ordinals

The Staircase

The diagonal's most famous deed is to draw a line no theory can cross: arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency. The line is real, but it is read wrong. It is not a wall at the edge of mathematics; it is the first step of a staircase that goes up forever. Add to a theory the one thing it cannot prove — its own consistency — and you have climbed exactly one rung; the new theory cannot prove its consistency, and so you climb again, and the rungs are well-ordered, and the height you have reached is an ordinal. This is the other face of the diagonal. Where it draws a ceiling on any single proof, it generates, read upward, the entire vertical of mathematical strength — an endless, exactly-measured ascent, with concrete theorems standing on its steps, climbed without end and, as it turns out, without a canonical route.

The Provenance laid the diagonal out sideways — among all the ways a fixed point can arise — and found it alone on the side that asks nothing of the world. This last mathematical descent stands it upright. The question is no longer where the diagonal sits but how high it reaches: granted that it bounds every theory from inside, what is on the other side of the bound, and how is the climb out of it structured? The Box opened the logic of a single provability operator and stopped at one box. Here that box is iterated — past Gödel, past the first failure, up a tower of theories each built from the last — and the surprise is that the climb is not chaos but an ordinal, the cleanest object in mathematics; that the abstract tower has rungs you can hold, ordinary theorems that are true and unprovable for an exactly measurable reason; and that whether the staircase reaches the top has an answer subtler than yes or no.

I · the first rung

Gödel's wall is a step

The second incompleteness theorem is told as an ending — the place arithmetic runs out. Read once more, with the next line attached, it is a beginning: the thing a theory cannot prove is the thing you add to climb.

Gödel's second theorem says that a consistent, computably axiomatized theory T extending arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency: T ⊬ Con(T). The collection has met this many times as a limit. But attend to what it does not say. It does not say Con(T) is false — if T is consistent, Con(T) is true. It says only that this particular truth lies just past T's reach. And a truth just past reach is an invitation: adjoin it.

the step
Let T₁ = T + Con(T). Then T₁ proves Con(T) — trivially, it is now an axiom — and if T is consistent so is T₁. So T₁ is strictly stronger than T: it settles a question T could not. One rung climbed.

And now the same theorem applies again, one floor up. T₁ is itself consistent, computably axiomatized, and extends arithmetic — so by Gödel, T₁ ⊬ Con(T₁). The wall has reappeared, raised by exactly one storey. Adjoin Con(T₁) to get T₂; the wall reappears again. There is no theory at which this stops, because the obstacle is not a feature of any particular theory but of the form of self-reference itself — every system rich enough to describe its own provability is blind to its own consistency, and curing the blindness produces a new system with the same blindness, forever. The diagonal does not put up one wall. It puts up a wall on every floor, which is the same as building a staircase. The limit, looked at twice, is a step; and once there is one step there is no last one.

II · the ladder

Climbing into the transfinite

Finite steps reach only the foothills. To keep climbing past them you must pass to a limit and continue — and indexing the continuation is what drags ordinals, and then ordinal notations, into the heart of the matter.

Iterate the step. From T build T₁, T₂, T₃, …, each the last plus its consistency. After all the finite stages, gather them: T_ω = ⋃ₙ Tₙ, the theory with every finite reflection as an axiom. It too is consistent and computably axiomatized, so Gödel applies and the climb resumes — T_{ω+1} = T_ω + Con(T_ω), and onward. To name the rungs past the limit you need the transfinite ordinals; this is the idea, arising the moment Gödel's theorem appeared, of iterating Gödelian extension along the ordinals, made precise by Turing's ordinal logics of 1939 and carried much further in Feferman's 1962 theory of transfinite progressions.

Two refinements turn the idea into a theory. First, consistency is only the mildest reflection. The general move is to add not just "T does not prove a contradiction" but "what T proves is true," in one of two strengths:

reflection, local and uniform
Local: Rfn(T) — for each sentence, Prov_T(⌜σ⌝) → σ. Uniform: RFN(T) — for each formula, ∀x ( Prov_T(⌜φ(ẋ)⌝) → φ(x) ). Consistency Con(T) = ¬Prov_T(⌜⊥⌝) is the weakest case. Uniform reflection is strictly stronger than local, which is strictly stronger than consistency.

Second, and more delicate: the theory at stage α must be computably axiomatized, so the climb cannot be indexed by abstract ordinals but only by ordinal notations — concrete codes for ordinals drawn from Kleene's 𝒪, the recursive system of names for the constructive ordinals. A progression is therefore not a single tower but an assignment of theories to notations, from which a sequence is extracted by choosing a path through 𝒪. That distinction — ordinal versus notation, tower versus path — looks like bookkeeping. It is, in fact, where the deepest surprise of the subject will hide, and §V will spring it. For now, the climb is underway and the natural question is how to measure how far it has gone.

III · the measure

The height of a theory is an ordinal

How far up does a given theory stand? Gentzen answered it for arithmetic, and the answer is the cleanest possible: a single ordinal, ε₀, below which the theory can climb and at which it cannot.

The measure is Gentzen's, from 1936, and it is the founding act of ordinal proof theory. Recall ε₀: the least ordinal closed under the tower of exponentials, ε₀ = sup{ω, ω^ω, ω^{ω^ω}, …}, equivalently the least α with ω^α = α. Gentzen showed that the consistency of arithmetic is provable by transfinite induction along ε₀ — and, exactly complementing it, that arithmetic itself can prove transfinite induction up to every α < ε₀ but not up to ε₀.

Gentzen — the ordinal of arithmetic
PRA + TI(ε₀) ⊢ Con(PA), while PA ⊢ TI(α) for every α < ε₀ and PA ⊬ TI(ε₀). So the proof-theoretic ordinal of arithmetic is exactly |PA| = ε₀: the precise height it can reach from below and cannot stand upon.

Two things in this deserve to be felt. The first is that the height is a number — an ordinal, the most transparent object mathematics has — so the vague notion "how strong is this theory" is made exact and comparable: stronger theories have larger ordinals, and the ordinal even pins down which functions the theory can prove total (for arithmetic, exactly those eventually dominated by the ε₀-level of the fast-growing hierarchy). The diagonal's wall has been replaced by a ruler.

The second is a correction the collection's honesty discipline insists on. It is often said that a theory's consistency can only be proved in a stronger theory. Gentzen's witnessing system shows this is false as stated. PRA + TI(ε₀) proves Con(PA), yet it does not contain PA — it lacks full induction for formulas with quantifiers. And PA does not contain it either, since PA cannot prove TI(ε₀). The two theories are incomparable: one buys reach into the transfinite at the cost of ordinary induction, the other the reverse. Consistency is not bought by sheer strength but by a different strength — more transfinite ladder, less of something else. The staircase is real, but you do not climb it by simply having more; you climb it by reaching further up the ordinals, and that is a specific currency.

IV · the concrete steps

Rungs you can hold

The staircase is not an abstraction floating above mathematics. It has visible steps — plain, finite, combinatorial theorems that are true and unprovable in arithmetic, and unprovable for the exact reason that their height is ε₀.

Gödel's own unprovable sentence is a coded curiosity, true but artificial — a formula engineered to talk about its own provability. For decades it was possible to believe that incompleteness, while real, never touched ordinary mathematics: that every natural theorem a working mathematician might state was, if true, provable. The 1970s and 80s ended that belief. There are entirely natural finite statements — about sequences of integers, about colorings, about a combinatorial monster — that are true, that mathematicians would state without a thought of logic, and that arithmetic cannot prove. They are the staircase made concrete.

Goodstein 1944every Goodstein sequence reaches 0. Write a number in hereditary base-n, bump every base, subtract one, repeat; the values explode astronomically, yet always return to zero. True — and unprovable in PA (Kirby–Paris, 1982).
Hydra 1982Hercules always kills the Hydra. Cut a head, it grows many more; yet every strategy wins in finite time. True — and unprovable in PA; the proof tracks a descending ε₀-ordinal.
Paris–Harrington 1977a strengthened finite Ramsey theorem — demanding a homogeneous set "relatively large." True — and unprovable in PA; the first natural mathematical statement shown independent of arithmetic.
why ε₀each implies the totality of a function growing like the ε₀-level of the fast-growing hierarchy F_{ε₀} — past every function PA proves total. The height is the obstruction.

This is the payoff that makes the ordinal measure more than proof-theorists' bookkeeping. The height ε₀ is not a technical artifact of one consistency proof; it is a threshold that ordinary mathematics keeps bumping into from the other side. Goodstein, Hercules, and Paris–Harrington each encode, in innocent finite language, a demand for transfinite induction up to ε₀ — exactly the rung Gentzen identified as the one arithmetic cannot stand upon. The unprovability is not a curiosity grafted on by a logician; it is the staircase showing through the floor of number theory. A statement is unprovable in PA precisely when its content reaches above PA's ordinal, and these reach, demonstrably, to ε₀.

V · the top

Does the staircase reach everything?

Climb far enough and can you prove every truth? The answer is the subtlest in the subject: yes — but only along specially chosen routes, and the choice smuggles the answers in. This is the surprise §II's bookkeeping was hiding.

If each rung settles a little more, perhaps the whole climb settles everything — perhaps iterated reflection, pushed far enough up the ordinals, proves every true sentence of arithmetic, and incompleteness is merely the condition of standing on any one step rather than the whole staircase. Astonishingly, in one precise sense this is true. And, just as precisely, it is a trap.

Feferman, 1962

It reaches everything

Iterating uniform reflection along the ordinal notations, every true sentence of arithmetic becomes provable at some stage. The progression of reflections is, in this sense, complete — there is a height in the notations from which any given truth is reached.

Only along chosen routes

The completeness is path-dependent. There are paths through the very same notations along which the progression stays incomplete — failing to prove even all true Π₁ sentences. Which truths you reach depends on which path you pick, not on how high you climb.

the route, not the height, decides

This is where the distinction flagged in §II — ordinal versus notation, tower versus path — turns out to carry the whole weight. An ordinal is a pure quantity; a notation is a recursive program that names it, and there can be cleverness in the program that the ordinal itself does not contain. Feferman's completeness exploits exactly that slack: it chooses, for each truth to be proved, a path through the notations whose structure already encodes the truth, so that "reaching the rung" and "knowing the answer" become the same act. The staircase does reach the top — but only if you are allowed to decide how to count the steps after you already know where you are going. Feferman would later call such notation-paths, with characteristic precision, the dishonest ones.

So the honest verdict is the one that respects the spirit of Gödel's theorem rather than evading it. Measured by genuine strength — by which ordinals a theory can prove well-founded, by which consistency statements it settles — the ascent has no top: every theory stands on a definite rung, and there is always a higher one, and no canonical climb captures all truths. Completeness is reachable only by smuggling, and smuggling is not climbing. Incompleteness is not defeated by the staircase; it is organized by it — turned from a single wall into an endless, well-ordered, ordinal-measured ascent that no honest route ever finishes.

VI · the height of mathematics

The limit, read upward, is the structure

Set this beside The Wall and the diagonal shows its two faces at once. There it was a ceiling — the boundary a method cannot cross. Here it is an engine — the same construction, read upward, building the entire vertical of mathematical strength.

Gather the climb. Incompleteness is not a wall but a staircase: every theory has a strictly stronger successor, got by adjoining the consistency it cannot prove; the successors run on into the transfinite, indexed by ordinal notations; the height a theory reaches is a definite ordinal, its proof-theoretic ordinal, exact and comparable — ε₀ for arithmetic, larger for stronger systems; the steps are not abstractions but ordinary finite theorems, true and unprovable for the precise reason that their content reaches above that ordinal; and the top, though reachable in a technical sense, is reachable only by smuggling truth into the notations, so that the honest ascent — measured by genuine strength — has no end at all. Self-reference, iterated, is the consistency-strength hierarchy of mathematics.

Now hold that against the companion descent. The Wall took the diagonal's limitative face to its sharpest point: the construction that draws every classical impossibility is itself bounded as a method — it relativizes, and so cannot cross into the questions that split over oracles. A ceiling on what the diagonal can do. The Staircase is the same construction seen from the opposite side. Read downward, against a single theory, the diagonal draws a wall — Gödel's wall, the limit of that theory's reach. Read upward, across the tower of theories, the very same wall, drawn on every floor, becomes the staircase that organizes all of mathematical strength into a well-ordered ascent measured by the ordinals. The diagonal does not merely bound. By bounding everywhere, it builds.

This is the reframing the collection has been climbing toward, and it cuts against the diagonal's reputation. The construction is filed, always, under the negative — the source of paradox, undecidability, incompleteness, the great catalogue of no. The Staircase shows the catalogue of no is, looked at from above, the skeleton of yes: the precise stratification of how much each theory can do, the ruler by which proof theory measures power, the spine along which Gentzen and his successors calibrate the strength of every formal system from arithmetic to large cardinals. The limits, stacked and well-ordered, are the structure. There is no separate positive theory of mathematical strength standing apart from the incompleteness phenomenon; the incompleteness phenomenon, iterated, is that theory. The diagonal draws one boundary, and by drawing it on every floor it constructs the staircase that is the backbone of the subject.

So the collection's wager extends one storey higher than it knew. One construction underlies the reach and the boundary of formal reasoning — yes; but the boundary, taken upward and well-ordered, is itself a reach: the diagonal that says no theory is enough is, in the same breath, the diagonal that says here, exactly, is how far each one gets — and the second saying, the ordinal ladder of strength, is the whole positive architecture of proof theory, generated by nothing but the limit, climbed without end and measured to the rung.

the wall on every floor is the staircase

Gödel's wall, drawn on every floor, is a staircase; its height is an ordinal; and the climb, honestly made, has no top. Which is not the failure of mathematics, but the shape of it.

The Staircase opens the vertical that The Box left at a single storey: the iteration of self-reference up the ordinals. It reads Gödel's second theorem as the first rung of an endless climb — adjoin the consistency a theory cannot prove and you ascend one step, forever; follows the climb into the transfinite through the Turing–Feferman reflection progressions, indexed not by ordinals but by their notations; measures the height of arithmetic as Gentzen did, exactly ε₀, with the honest correction that the witnessing theory is incomparable with PA rather than simply stronger; finds the staircase's concrete steps in the natural independent statements of Goodstein, the Hydra, and Paris–Harrington; and springs the subtlest result in the subject — that the ascent reaches every truth (Feferman) but only along non-canonical notation-paths that smuggle the answers in (Feferman–Spector), so that the honest climb has no end.
