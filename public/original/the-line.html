The architecture of an incomplete bargain

The Line

The employment contract is necessarily incomplete: it sells a capacity to labor over time that no document can fully specify. Wage-and-hour law is the state's mandatory completion of that contract — and it completes it the only way a continuum yields discrete dollars, by cutting. There are four cuts, and they are not parallel but a pipeline; uncertainty at any stage compounds down the line; every cut faces one inescapable trilemma; and where each cut falls is not a technicality but a choice about who bears the cost.

§ 1 · the ground

Why the bargain is unfinished

Before any line is drawn, ask what makes work resist the cut. The answer is not metaphysical but institutional — and it tells us exactly where the four boundaries must fall.

A sale of bread ends when the loaf changes hands. The employment contract never closes that way, because it does not buy a finished thing — it buys a capacity to labor over time, exercised later, under direction not yet given. Coase saw why the firm exists at all: some coordination is too contingent to write down, so the parties substitute an open-ended relation of authority for a complete schedule of trades. Simon made the form precise — the worker agrees to accept the employer's direction within a zone, in exchange for a wage — which is to say the contract is deliberately incomplete, a frame around a space it leaves blank.

That blankness is not a drafting failure to be cured by better lawyers. It is constitutive. No instrument can specify, in advance, every minute that will count as working, every task that will fall inside the job, every gradation of effort, every contingency of the day. The wage is paid against time-and-availability whose content is settled only as it is lived. And because one party writes the rules and keeps the records, the blank space is also a space of power.

Wage-and-hour law is what fills that space — not by agreement, which is why its core protections cannot be waived, but by command. It is the mandatory backstop that completes the contract at exactly the margins the parties left open or that advantage would exploit. And the completion has a shape: the law must take heterogeneous, continuous human activity and render it in one homogeneous metric — dollars per hour. Commensuration of a continuum always leaves a residue at the boundary, and the boundaries are precisely the four dimensions along which the contract was left unfinished.

Why by command, and not by a default the parties could contract around? Because the incompleteness here is not symmetric, and three features of the wage relation make a merely optional floor worthless. The labor is sunk and perishable: the worker supplies it before any dispute about its boundaries can arise and cannot withdraw it after, so she is held up ex post — the textbook problem of a relationship-specific investment with no outside option once made. The information is one-sided: the employer writes the schedule and keeps the records, so the worker frequently cannot even know she was underpaid. And waiver carries an externality: the worker who bargains away the floor lowers it for the next, because firms compete on labor cost — a race to the bottom no individual can refuse alone. Each feature defeats a default rule, and — this is the part worth holding onto — each dictates the shape of a particular cut downstream. The holdup is why coverage is mandatory and misclassification actionable; the asymmetry is why the proof cut puts the burden on the record-keeper; the externality is why the floor is fixed collectively and cannot be contracted around (Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil; Barrentine). The architecture of the doctrine is already legible in the economics of the bargain.

§ 2 · the pipeline

Four cuts, in series

The boundaries are not parallel problems but a pipeline of quantizers — each forcing the continuum through a discrete legal category — and the error each introduces propagates and compounds into dollars downstream.

Here is the operation the four cuts share — what makes them one problem and not four. Each forces a continuous, many-dimensional reality through a discrete legal category: how economically integrated a worker is, collapsed to employee-or-not; how much of the continuum is work, collapsed to counted-or-not; what an hour is worth, collapsed to a single dollar figure; how certain we are, collapsed to proven-or-not. That operation has a name in information theory — quantization, the encoding of a continuous signal into a finite alphabet — and §7 shows it carries an unavoidable cost. The four are not quite uniform: three quantize the continuum into a decision — in or out, counted or not, proven or not — while valuation quantizes it into a measurement, a dollar figure, and that lone difference is why self-reference surfaces only at the price (Cut III), where the rate is read off a total that already contains it. First, though, the structure. The cuts compose into a pipeline, and the order is not incidental. Coverage is a gate: a covered employee, of a covered enterprise, outside any exemption — a switch that, if open, zeroes everything after it. Compensability defines the domain: which slices of the continuum enter the count as hours. Valuation prices each unit: the regular rate. Proof allocates the residual doubt about all three. Schematically, exposure is a product — coverage, times the compensable hours, times the rate, scaled by the penalties — and because it is a product, error does not add. It multiplies.

This is the structural fact the technicalities obscure. A twenty-percent dispute about hours and a fifteen-percent dispute about the rate do not make a thirty-five-percent quarrel; multiplied, they open a band of exposure roughly twice as wide at the top as at the bottom. The gate, being a zero-or-one, can extinguish a vast sum or unlock it on a single ruling. And the proof cut decides who stands inside that compounded band. Run the pipeline below: set the disputes, open or close the gate, and watch a modest disagreement at each stage become an immodest range of dollars — the range within which every case is actually settled.

cut I · the gate

Who is inside the relation

Coverage is conjunctive — an employee, of a covered enterprise, not exempt — and each conjunct is its own contested line. The same worker can fall on either side, not because the facts differ but because the tests integrate them differently.

Set the worker's reality — three tests, three ways of cutting one spectrum

three cuts hiding in one  The gate is usually spoken of as a single question — employee or not — but it is three. First, employee versus independent contractor, governed above. Second, and almost always assumed away for large firms, enterprise or individual coverage: the FLSA reaches employees of enterprises engaged in commerce above a revenue floor, or individuals themselves engaged in commerce. Third, and most heavily litigated in practice, the exemptions — and the exemptions are a hybrid cut in miniature. A white-collar exemption requires a salary basis, a salary level (a bright-line dollar threshold, a pure rule, and one currently in flux as the 2024 increase was struck down and relitigated), and a duties test — "primary duty," "discretion and independent judgment with respect to matters of significance" — which is irreducibly evaluative. The employer bears the burden of proving the exemption, and the interpretive thumb has shifted: Encino Motorcars v. Navarro (2018) retired the old canon that exemptions are "narrowly construed" in favor of a "fair reading," nudging marginal cases toward exemption.

strategic stakes — the gate is dispositive and class-wide

Because coverage multiplies the whole pipeline by zero or one, it is the highest-leverage attack in the field: a single classification ruling flips an entire workforce at once, which is why misclassification is pleaded first and certified as a class. In California, prong B makes many gig and platform arrangements nearly unwinnable for the hiring entity — if the workers do the company's core work, A and C rarely matter. Exemption fights, by contrast, turn on the duties standard, which resists summary judgment precisely because it is evaluative — and a claim that survives summary judgment is a claim that settles. The defense response runs the other way: structure genuine independence (and document it), and engineer roles to satisfy the duties test cleanly rather than litigate it.

cut II · the domain

Which time counts

Compensability is two cuts that are constantly conflated: a categorical one — is this activity work at all? — and a quantum one — even if it is, is the amount too small to count? They are sequential, and they can diverge.

the categorical cut · 0 or 1
Is the activity compensable work? Governed by the principal-activity and integral-and-indispensable test: an intrinsic element of the job that the worker cannot dispense with. Donning required protective gear qualifies (Steiner); a post-shift anti-theft screening does not (Busk). Once the first principal activity occurs, the continuous workday runs to the last (IBP v. Alvarez).
the quantum cut · how much
If it is work, is the amount trivial? Governed by de minimis: split-second bits may be disregarded, weighed by regularity, aggregate size, and the difficulty of recording (Mt. Clemens; Lindow). California sets this threshold to zero for work that regularly recurs (Troester), so the quantum cut all but disappears there.

why the conflation matters  Running the two cuts together is the most common analytical error in compensability, and it changes outcomes. Treating boot-up as "de minimis, so non-compensable" skips the prior question — whether it is a principal activity that opens the workday, which, if so, makes the subsequent walking and waiting compensable regardless of the boot-up's own size. The categorical cut sets the workday's boundaries; the quantum cut only trims the edges within them. Troester is best read not as inventing a new category but as refusing the quantum cut for recurring work — leaving the categorical analysis to do the heavy lifting, as it should.

strategic stakes — off-the-clock exposure is assembled from both cuts

Plaintiffs build off-the-clock claims on two rails. The categorical rail: rounding that systematically nets against employees (lawful only if neutral — See's Candy), meal breaks auto-deducted but not truly duty-free, pre- and post-shift tasks that are principal activities. The quantum rail: aggregate the seconds across the workforce and the limitations period until they are unmistakably substantial — Troester's arithmetic, ninety seconds a day into hours a year. The liability hinge is the employer's actual or constructive knowledge of the unrecorded work; and because modern timekeeping can capture nearly anything, the administrative-difficulty rationale that once justified de minimis is eroding. Defense: audit rounding for neutrality, make auto-deductions rebuttable and policed, and — above all — capture the time, which moots the quantum cut entirely.

cut III · the price

What a unit is worth

Overtime is one-and-a-half times the regular rate — but the regular rate is a value that depends on total pay, and total pay depends on overtime, which depends on the regular rate. When pay refers to pay, the price must solve for itself.

a price that solves for itself  Fix the wage in dollars and the loop is degenerate: the rate is just pay over hours. But make a bonus a percentage of total compensation — including the overtime that the bonus itself generates — and the definition closes on its own output. The lawful figure is then the solution of an equation, T = (wH + p·T)·c, where c folds the overtime premium into straight-time remuneration. Because that map is an affine contraction — each pass multiplies the gap by p·c < 1 — it has a unique fixed point, and naïve iteration walks straight to it: pay the bonus, see the rate rise, recompute the overtime, which enlarges the bonus, and so on, the corrections shrinking geometrically to wH·c / (1 − p·c). The percentage-of-earnings safe harbor of § 778.210 is simply that closed form pre-solved, which is why it needs no recomputation at all.

It is worth being exact about the kinship and its limit. This is a Banach fixed point — a contraction on the real line — not the diagonal, categorical fixed point that forces incompleteness and recursion in the companion essay on self-reference. The resonance is structural: in both, a definition is permitted to reference its own result, and the lawful or consistent value is the one that reproduces itself. The mathematics differs; the shape of the problem does not.

strategic stakes — the rate is a damages multiplier

An understated regular rate underpays every overtime hour, for every affected worker, across the entire limitations period — and is then doubled as liquidated damages unless the employer proves good faith (29 U.S.C. § 216(b)). A systematic rate error of a few percent — a nondiscretionary bonus quietly left out of the rate — is therefore one of the largest exposures in the field, far out of proportion to its per-hour size. Plaintiffs audit pay codes for any nondiscretionary remuneration excluded from the rate; defendants either fold bonuses into the § 778.210 structure or make them genuinely discretionary, and document the basis for any fluctuating-workweek arrangement, whose conditions most employers fail. California raises the stakes again: under Ferra v. Loews (2021), even meal- and rest-period premiums are owed at the regular rate of compensation, nondiscretionary pay included — not the bare base wage — so the same rate error reverberates beyond overtime into the premium-pay claims.

cut IV · the residue

What is owed when no one can say

The first three cuts run through the world; this one runs through our knowledge of it. Because the edges are fuzzy and the duty to record falls on one party, the last cut decides who bears the doubt the others leave behind.

The compounded band from §2, now allocated — toggle whether the employer kept records

employer's records

the engine, and its precise limit  The burden-shift is the plaintiff's evidentiary engine: the employer's own record-keeping failure converts the worker's testimony into a prima facie case and forces the defense to disprove a negative. But its precision matters in both directions. It does not manufacture liability — a plaintiff who cannot show that some uncompensated work occurred gets nothing, however poor the records. What it does is relax the exactness of quantum once liability is shown, shifting to the wrongdoer the risk of the very imprecision its silence created. That is why, in the pipeline of §2, missing records push recovery toward the top of the compounded band: the doubt the first three cuts generated is resolved against the party who could have dispelled it and chose not to.

strategic stakes — the burden-shift sets settlement value

Coupled with representative proof, Mt. Clemens is what makes wage-and-hour class and PAGA actions viable, and it drives settlement toward the high end of the band — the defendant, not the plaintiff, bears the fuzziness the records would have removed. In California, the lever is sturdier still: manageability cannot be used to strike a PAGA claim wholesale (Estrada, 2024), and while arbitration can carve off the individual PAGA claim, the representative claim survives (Viking River; Adolph). The entire defense reduces to one imperative — records, records, records: accurate, contemporaneous timekeeping collapses the band to a point, restores the ordinary burden of proof, and denies the plaintiff the inference that would otherwise do all the work.

§ 7 · the constraint

The cost of the cut

The earlier edition asserted the trilemma; here it is derived from a single decomposition — and shown to turn on a quantity the triangle hides: how much reliable fact the system can bear.

In §2 each cut became a quantizer of the continuum; here is what quantizing costs, and why no cut can pay nothing. Treat a legal rule as an estimator — a procedure that takes the facts of a case and returns the outcome the law is after. Statistical learning theory decomposes any estimator's expected error into three parts, and they are exactly our three corners. Approximation error — how far the best rule of its kind can sit from the truth — is infidelity, and it is large for a coarse rule that cannot bend to the cases. Estimation error — how much the rule's output swings with the particular facts a tribunal happens to find — is indeterminacy, and it is large for a rich rule read from sparse evidence. And complexity — how much the rule must say to specify itself — is the cost of administering it. The corners are not three analogies bolted together; they are the three terms of one decomposition.

What forbids having all three small at once is a bound, not a mood. To shrink approximation error you must enrich the rule — move it toward a standard, give it more degrees of freedom to fit the cases. But a richer rule has more ways to go wrong on limited evidence, so its estimation error grows; structural-risk theory makes the growth precise — estimation error rises with complexity and falls with the quantity of reliable fact. So fidelity demands complexity, and complexity, unless paid for in evidence, buys indeterminacy. The only escape is to spend the third resource outright: a detailed code drives both errors down by enumerating the answer for every case — effectively supplying its own evidence — and pays for it in sheer complexity, the administrability cost. Determinate, faithful, administrable: hold any two, surrender the third. That is the trilemma, and it is now an identity with a bound behind it, not a slogan.

Two quantities therefore govern where the optimal cut falls, and the second is the one the triangle hides. The first is the heterogeneity of the domain — how varied the cases are — and a varied domain punishes a rule's bias, pushing the optimum toward a standard. The second is the evidentiary budget — how much reliable fact the system can actually ascertain and process — and this is nothing other than what the proof cut of §6 governs. When facts are cheap and certain, a standard's estimation error is tamed and the law can afford fidelity; when proof is dear, a rule's determinacy is worth more than a standard the tribunal cannot reliably apply. This is why the case for a compensability standard strengthened exactly as timekeeping technology improved: Troester reasoned that the old administrative-difficulty excuse for the de minimis rule no longer holds once employers can capture the time. The proof cut does not merely allocate residual doubt downstream — the cost of proof feeds back to set the optimal shape of every other cut. The triangle is traversed, not occupied

The trilemma is not a one-time choice; rules and standards migrate. A bright-line rule, applied to the cases it betrays, accretes exceptions to cure the unfairness — and a rule riddled with exceptions is a standard. A standard, applied case after case, hardens into presumptions and safe harbors as courts hunt for predictability — and a standard reduced to presumptions is a rule. The field drifts, perpetually, between the corners; reform is the deliberate relocation of a doctrine across the triangle. Watch one such move below: California's AB5 took worker classification from the open Borello standard and dragged it toward the categorical ABC rule — buying determinacy and administrability, paying in fidelity, on a bet that the domain was uniform enough to bear it. The doctrines scatter across the space because the space has no stable centre to settle in.

§ 8 · the game

The rule invites the move

A cut is not drawn against passive facts. The regulated party rearranges itself around the line — and that response changes what each cut actually costs, in ways the boundary error alone never shows.

A cut is drawn against a party who will rearrange the facts to its advantage, which makes the setting of any line a mechanism-design problem: the regulator must choose the quantizer knowing it will be optimised against. The sharpest consequence falls on bright lines. Wherever crossing the line yields a discrete benefit — a salary at the threshold buys the exemption, an hour under forty avoids the premium, a contract just clearing prong B escapes employee status — the regulated party crowds the favourable edge. The employer does not pay what the job is worth or schedule the hours the work needs; it pays exactly the line and schedules exactly to it. Economists call the pile-up bunching, and it is measurable: a spike sits in the salary distribution precisely at the exemption threshold, with a hole carved just beneath. Raise the stakes below and watch the spike grow — and switch to a standard to watch it vanish.

Two consequences fall out. First, a rule's true bias exceeds its boundary error: beyond the cases it misclassifies, it distorts the cases it does not, paying a crowd of workers exactly the line rather than their worth — and a standard, having no line to crowd, escapes this particular cost even as it incurs the other. Second, the line is where the law is made and unmade. The cases that litigate are the close ones — clear cases settle, as Priest and Klein observed — so the disputes that reach courts cluster at the very boundary the cut cannot get right, and the doctrine is forever built, and forever destabilised, at its own edge. The regulator's question is therefore not "where is the correct line" but "where is the line that survives the response it provokes" — which is why reform is less a discovery than a move in a game that does not end.

strategic stakes — the misclassification perfect storm

The cleanest place to watch the four cuts compound is the salaried employee misclassified as exempt. When the exemption fails, every cut breaks the same way at once. The gate flips — the worker was nonexempt all along. The price must be reconstructed from scratch: there was never an hourly rate, so the parties fight over whether the salary bought straight time only (a high regular rate, full overtime on top) or all hours worked (the fluctuating-workweek theory, a much lower one). The domain is wide open, because an employee treated as exempt was never clocked — there are no time records at all — and the residue cut hands that absence to the plaintiff, who proves her hours by reasonable inference under Mt. Clemens. Then willfulness doubles the award and adds the third year. The exposures do not add; they multiply, and one classification error becomes the largest number in the case — the pipeline of §2 at full compounding, in the most ordinary fact pattern in the field.

§ 9 · synthesis

The line is a distribution

The last move is to see that where each cut falls is never neutral. To draw a line through the continuum of work is to decide who bears the cost of its being drawn there.

Assemble the architecture. Wage-and-hour law is the mandatory completion of a contract that is constitutively incomplete, executed as a pipeline of four quantizers — gate, domain, price, residue — each paying the same rate–distortion price, with error compounding down the line and the proof cut charging the residue to whoever could have recorded it and did not. That is the structure. Two things follow, one strategic and one distributive.

The strategic one makes the pipeline operational. A case's settlement value is, to a first approximation, the expected judgment — the probability of liability times the damages if liable — adjusted for each side's litigation cost and appetite for risk. Lay that over the cuts and every term has an address. The probability is fixed almost entirely at the binary cuts, the gate and the categorical compensability question, which a case wins or loses outright. The expected damages are the midpoint of the compounded band: hours times rate times the penalties. The risk the parties price is the band's width. And the proof cut moves two terms at once — missing records both raise the chance of establishing the violation and slide the conditional damages toward the top of the band — which is why the burden-shift is worth more than it appears, and why the presence or absence of timekeeping, more than any single legal question, sets where a case settles. Willfulness then scales the whole figure twice, doubling the award and adding the third year, a combined multiplier near three on everything beneath it. The map from doctrine to dollars is not loose; it is this product, and a litigator who knows which cut moves which factor knows where the money is.

The distributive one is the deeper, and it begins by noticing what the optimisation in §7 quietly assumed. To speak of an optimal cut — the one that minimises expected loss — is to presuppose a loss function: a settled account of the target the rule should track, against which approximation error is even defined. But the target of wage-and-hour law is not settled. Is it to pay workers for time actually worked, to shift surplus toward labour, to halt a race to the bottom, or to deter employer opportunism? These aims do not coincide, and they price the same error differently — so the loss-minimising cut is optimal only relative to a chosen objective, and choosing the objective is itself the distributive act. The machinery of §7 is real, but it runs only after someone has fixed the loss function, and fixing the loss function is not a technical step; it is the whole political question, entered as a premise. With that in view, the location of each cut is plainly not neutral. A determinate bright line favours neither labour nor capital as such; it favours whoever benefits from where it is drawn — a daily-overtime rule transfers to workers, a high salary threshold that exempts more of them transfers away. An open standard does not split the difference; it relocates the contest to adjudication, advantaging the repeat player and the side that can bear delay and litigation risk. A detailed code advantages those who can afford the experts to read it. There is no neutral place to cut a continuum, because every cut both prices labour and allocates the risk of the pricing — and those are the very stakes the parties were fighting over.

This is why the lines never hold still. The independent-contractor rule, the salary threshold, the conditions on the fluctuating workweek — each flips with the administration, not because the law is unsettled in some remediable way, but because each is a contested position on a trilemma whose every point allocates between capital and labor. The technicalities you began with — the fractions, the exemptions, the counted and uncounted minutes — are the surface of a distributive struggle conducted in the grammar of line-drawing. The companion essay showed that self-reference forces every system rich enough to describe itself to surrender one of universality, negation, and consistency. This one closes on its sibling: the wage relation forces every legal order that would price labor to surrender one of determinacy, fidelity, and administrability — and that surrender is never abstract, because it always names a party to bear it.

determinacy · fidelity · administrability

The continuum of work will not divide itself; the dollar insists that it must. Each cut the law makes is a position taken — knowing the cost — on an impossibility, and the bill is passed down the pipeline to whoever could not record their way out of it.

On method and limits. This is an analytical essay, not legal advice, and it abstracts deliberately. It states federal law under the Fair Labor Standards Act with California as the recurring contrast; the rules differ by jurisdiction and shift with each administration — the independent-contractor regulation, the salary-level threshold, and the conditions on the fluctuating workweek have all changed repeatedly and remain contested. The classification model approximates as a tally a totality test that expressly forbids mechanical counting; treat its verdicts as illustrations of how holistic balancing and conjunctive screening diverge, not as predictions. The regular-rate figures assume a nonexempt employee and the § 207(e) exclusions; the fluctuating workweek carries strict conditions not shown.

On the two fixed points, and the one decomposition. The recursion in Cut III is a contraction on the real line — a Banach fixed point — mathematically distinct from the diagonal, categorical fixed point of the companion essay on self-reference, with which it shares only its shape: a definition permitted to reference its own output, resolved at the value that reproduces itself. The trilemma is re-founded here on a single framework — the bias–variance–complexity decomposition of statistical learning, bounded by structural risk — in which fidelity is low approximation error, determinacy is low estimation error, and administrability is low complexity, and the three cannot be small together while the evidence is finite. The decomposition is an exact identity and the structural-risk relation an exact bound; the modelling step — treating a legal rule as an estimator of the law's target, a doctrine as a quantizer of the continuum of facts, and the proof regime as the supply of evidence — is where the rigour meets the metaphor. The decomposition is a theorem; that the law obeys it is an argument, though a strong one, and one that presupposes a loss function the law does not in fact settle (see §9). The §7 curves use stylised constants chosen for legibility; what they show exactly is the two comparative statics — that the loss-minimising cut slides toward the standard as the domain grows more heterogeneous, or as the evidentiary budget grows. What is exact throughout is the mathematics of the rate and the holdings cited; what is argued is that the field is one structure, and that the structure is the cost of quantizing a continuum under limited proof.
